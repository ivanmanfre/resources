02 / The evidence

Find a signal. Keep its limits.

Most viral-post lists reward the biggest accounts. I first compare each post with the same writer’s usual result. Then I ask whether any repeated pattern is relevant to the post in front of me.

An illustration, not a study result

The smaller post can be the bigger outlier.

Big account: 800 likes
Usual for that author: 8001×
Small founder: 60 likes
Usual for that author: 154×
Research score = likes + 3 × reposts
Outlier ratio = post score ÷ that author’s median
Follower count is not used as the denominator. Reposts are weighted in this historical measure; the ratio describes engagement against a baseline, not reach or buyer value.

03 / Openings

Choose the opening for this post.

An opening has to make sense for the point you can support and the reader you want. A short line, a question or a story is not automatically good or bad.

In the October writing study, openings drawn from something an author did, saw or measured were associated with higher engagement in the pooled sample and in the automation niche. Other niches differed. A later hook study found real personal moments among winners in selected peer comparisons. In an October 17 check limited to work-related posts, that advantage weakened. The same method could not reliably rank Ivan’s own posts. Keep the opening true to the work instead of adding a personal moment for the pattern.

The practical check: what is this post trying to do, what really happened, and does the body deliver what the first lines promise? If the answer needs a fabricated moment or an unsupported number, change the opening.

Two legitimate ways in
If you did the workLead with the thing you actually found.

A task, decision, result or specific observation gives the reader something real to follow.

If you are explaining a buyer problemStart with the decision they face.

Use the language of that decision, then bring your proof into the body. Do not invent a first-person story to fit a pattern.

These are editorial routes, not ranked templates. The research cannot tell you which one will bring clients.

04 / Body and proof

Make the opening pay off.

A post can get attention with a good first line and still leave the reader with nothing. The useful part is the fact, example or reasoning that follows.

In the October 9 study, story-carried bodies were associated with higher engagement overall and more strongly in the automation sample. That is a description of those posts, not a requirement to turn every explanation into a story. Length bands varied too. Use enough space to make the point clear.

  • Show the work or source behind a number.
  • Keep someone else’s outcome with that person.
  • Use a list when the material is a list.
  • Leave a claim out when your notes cannot support it.
A real public outlier

“We were Clay’s largest user at one point in time, hitting their platform 17.3 million times per week.”

That post opens with a specific piece of the author’s work and then explains what happened. Its strong result against the author’s baseline makes it worth reading. It cannot prove that this opening caused the result.

05 / Endings

End where the post needs to end.

The earlier closer study did not establish one ending that wins across posts. An ask for comments may bring comments; that does not make it the right end for every idea.

Pick the goal before the last line.

Some posts should finish on the observation. Some should invite a specific conversation. A resource post can give a direct link. Check the real action you want and keep the ask proportionate.

06 / The weekly loop

A useful Monday with the data

Make the research a weekly habit: use it to start a decision, then write from the work and facts only you know.

1

Pull

Find recent posts that beat each author’s usual result. Keep the niche, author and original source beside each example.

2

Pick

Choose a few worth studying for different reasons: the angle, the proof, the structure or the reader response.

3

Translate

Take one useful idea into your own work. Write from your notes, customer questions and supported facts.

4

Check

Mark every number and claim with its source. Check the opening against the body, then decide what the post is asking for.

You can do this manually. List the authors your buyers follow, record a recent run of posts from each, calculate each author’s median, and look for unusually strong posts. The plugin makes a slice of the public research easier to query when you are already working in ChatGPT.

09 / What the research cannot tell you

Useful evidence has edges.

  • Engagement is one outcome.Likes and reposts show how a post did in that historical measure. They do not measure qualified leads, booked calls or revenue.
  • A pattern is not a command.Observed differences can reflect topic, author, format and timing. None tells us that one wording change will cause the same result for you.
  • Your source still matters most.The plugin cannot invent a client moment, account result or buyer insight you have not supplied. It should say when the covered data does not fit your niche.
  • The public tool is narrow on purpose.It offers read-only research tasks in its current covered niches. It is not a publisher, scheduler, personal-account analytics tool or full Content BRAIN service.

Method note: the October 9 study analyzed 10,629 posts from 140 authors in three niches, using author-relative controls. The separate October 16 study analyzed 17,322 public posts across four research cohorts, with a held-out author comparison of extreme same-author post pairs. Both use likes plus three times reposts as the historical engagement measure. Their results are observational. They do not establish causal lift, see-more clicks or buyer conversion.
